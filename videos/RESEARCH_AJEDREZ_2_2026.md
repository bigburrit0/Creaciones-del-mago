## (a) The customer's own words (snippet level)

- **Praised:** the detail and design ("Amazing detail on every piece", an Etsy review summary, secondary). The look as a whole. "Avoids worry about battle scars at the club" (chess.com forum, quote from a snippet).
- **Feared or criticised (makers and forum posts, opinion):**
  - pieces that are **too light** (the most repeated complaint; makers add steel balls or weights);
  - **fragile thin parts** (crowns, spears, ears);
  - pieces that **fall over** (felt and weight in the base help);
  - **visible layer lines** up close;
  - colours that differ from the photo;
  - the **scale** against the board;
  - "cheap plastic" as a prejudice.
- **Questions people ask before buying** (derived; no buyer question text could be read):
  1. How heavy is it, and will it stay standing?
  2. Is the base felted?
  3. What size for my board?
  4. Solid or hollow?
  5. Are the layer lines visible?
  6. Will the colours match the photo?
  7. How fragile is it?
  8. How long does it take, and how is it packed?

**Implications:** never claim "heavy", "solid", "felted" or "smooth" until the shop confirms it. Questions for the owner are listed under "What the shop should tell me" below.

## (c) The psychology of a considered, handmade gift (published studies)

- **The labour illusion:** Buell & Norton 2011, *Management Science* 57(9) (data). Seeing the work raises perceived value even when the result is identical. Buell, Kim & Tsay 2017, *Management Science* 63(6), on operational transparency (data): customers who see the process rate the effort and quality higher.
- **When it backfires:** Morales 2005, *JCR* 31(4) (data). Visible effort raises willingness to pay through gratitude, and the effect **disappears when the effort looks like a sales tactic**. Padding, failed prints or stalling read as waste (opinion).
- **"I designed it myself":** Franke, Schreier & Kaiser 2010, *Management Science* 56(1) (data). Self-designed products earn a higher willingness to pay. Its transfer to a gift *for someone else* is untested.
- **The handmade effect:** Fuchs, Schreier & van Osselaer 2015, *Journal of Marketing* 79(2) (data). Handmade conveys love, and the premium is strongest for gifts to loved ones. There is **no solid evidence that buyers see 3D printing as handmade**, so the love has to come from the person ("one person, made to order"), not from the machine.
- **Choice overload is conditional:**
  - Scheibehenne et al. 2010, a meta-analysis, found a mean effect near zero (data).
  - Chernev et al. 2015 found it matters with complex sets and unsure buyers (data).
  - So give two simple decisions (the colours of each side) and defaults for the rest.
- **The giver's anxiety:**
  - Flynn & Adams 2009 (data): recipients value modest gifts about as much as expensive ones.
  - Gino & Flynn 2011 (data): explicit requests help givers. Ask "¿para quién es?" early.
  - Wooten 2000: the anxiety centres on "will they like it".

## (d) The critic: why "Una más" (the first draft) could flop

1. A non-player can't read the first 3 s. "32 piezas" and "una pieza más y paramos" have no context. Name the product and make the 32 visible with a counter.
2. Nothing says "this is for sale" until second 13.
3. The CTA asks "¿medieval o vikingo?" after showing only viking pieces.
4. "Comentá y te mando las fotos" offers a weak reward. Offer price and date.
5. An unplugged printer that keeps printing reads as a glitch, and the "puff" reads as smoke or fire to makers. If it's magic, show the magic: El Mago's gesture and sparkles.
6. "Muchas horas" alone reads as slow or expensive. Frame the time as care.
7. Process time-lapses are a crowded format. The gag and the character are the difference.
8. Repetition with reel 1 and a dead loop at 16-19 s.

Evidence is opinion plus vendor surveys (Clutch, n=483: 55% stop for a compelling hook, 43% for striking visuals; secondary). The critic also asked for a price on screen; **rejected**: the shop's rule is price by WhatsApp.

## Rules that change (they apply from reel 2 on)

1. **Frame 1 names the product in words** ("Ajedrez vikingo impreso en 3D"), and numbers are made visible (a counter, 1/32 → 32/32).
2. **Show the work honestly:** piece by piece, layers rising, with time passing shown by the light (day → night → dawn), and no failed prints. Time appears only as a true fact ("un juego completo lleva muchas horas de impresora").
3. **Magic must read as magic:** El Mago's gesture plus sparkles. No smoke or puffs near a printer.
4. **The CTA asks only about what the reel showed**, and rewards the comment with something real: "te paso precio y fecha".
5. **The caption frames choices as two decisions** (the colours of each side), says "las piezas; el tablero va aparte", and the DM asks "¿para quién es?".
6. **No claims about weight, felt, solidity or finish** until the shop confirms them.
